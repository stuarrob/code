\begin{abstract}
Long-only smart-beta ETF strategy operated against a live IB Gateway account. Selects positions from a filtered universe of $\sim$2{,}000 US-listed ETFs by ranking on a weighted geometric mean of four factors (momentum, quality, low-volatility, value). Top 20 held with exponential rank weights; 10\% trailing stops on every position; drift-triggered rebalance ($\sim$6/yr). This document is the operator's reading surface and takes precedence over marketing summaries; the code in \texttt{src/} is authoritative for behaviour, this document is authoritative for intent.
\end{abstract}

\section{Strategy Mechanics}
\label{sec:mechanics}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Universe Selection}

Starting from the full IB-listed ETF universe (\textasciitilde 5{,}000 tickers), the filter chain is:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item History requirement: $\geq$252 trading days of price data.
    \item Data quality: $<$10\% missing daily bars.
    \item Leveraged/inverse exclusion: leveraged ETFs are removed by ticker prefix and issuer classification.
    \item Result: $\sim$2{,}000 candidates on which factor scores are computed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Factor Framework --- What the Four Factors Are and Why}

The four factors below are the AQR-canonical smart-beta four: momentum, quality, low-volatility, value. They were chosen because they are the four with the largest and most robust empirical premia over the widest range of markets and time periods studied in the academic literature. AQR chose these because after decades of empirical work they are the return sources that survive out-of-sample testing, transaction costs, and factor-crowding. The strategy does not attempt to identify novel factors.

\subsubsection{Momentum (target 35\% weight)}

\begin{equation}
\text{Momentum}_i = \frac{P_{i,t-21} - P_{i,t-252}}{P_{i,t-252}}
\end{equation}

12-month return, with the most recent 21 days omitted. Winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentile.

\textbf{Why:} Assets that have outperformed over the prior year tend to continue outperforming over the next year. The 1-month skip avoids the short-term reversal effect that would contaminate the trend signal. Established by Jegadeesh \& Titman (1993); the skip refinement from Novy-Marx (2012).

\textbf{Why AQR uses it:} The most-studied, most-robust factor after value, and the largest-premium factor in the last decade. Works across equity, futures, and FX. Fails during trend reversals (2022 tech drawdown, 2000 dot-com peak).

\subsubsection{Quality (target 30\% weight)}

Composite of three z-score-normalised components on 252-day windows:

\begin{align}
\text{Sharpe Ratio} &= \frac{\mu_r - r_f}{\sigma_r} \sqrt{252} \quad (40\% \text{ weight}) \\
\text{Drawdown Resilience} &= -1 \times \text{MaxDD} \quad (30\% \text{ weight}) \\
\text{Return Stability} &= -1 \times \sigma_r \sqrt{252} \quad (30\% \text{ weight})
\end{align}

\textbf{Why:} Assets that produce stable, high risk-adjusted returns tend to continue doing so. Established by Asness, Frazzini \& Pedersen (2019).

\textbf{Why AQR uses it:} Quality captures the defensive-equity premium. Correlated with low-volatility but not identical --- quality picks up profitability and stability of \emph{returns}, low-vol picks up stability of \emph{prices}. Both belong in the portfolio because they hedge different aspects of stress.

\subsubsection{Low-Volatility (target 20\% weight)}

\begin{equation}
\text{Volatility}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_{60d,i} \times \sqrt{252}}
\end{equation}

Inverse of annualised realised volatility over 60 trading days. Higher score for lower-volatility ETFs.

\textbf{Why:} The low-volatility anomaly --- lower-volatility assets have historically produced higher risk-adjusted returns than higher-volatility assets, contradicting the CAPM. Baker, Bradley \& Wurgler (2011).

\textbf{Why AQR uses it:} Two reasons. First, the low-vol premium is real and persistent. Second, low-vol allocations reduce portfolio-level whipsaw, which matters more than arithmetic-mean metrics suggest in a compounding portfolio (see \S\ref{sec:weights}).

\subsubsection{Value (target 15\% weight) --- currently degraded}

\begin{equation}
\text{Value}_i = -1 \times \text{Expense Ratio}_i
\end{equation}

\textbf{This is not a value factor.} It is a low-fee preference. A real value factor uses fundamental multiples (P/E, P/B, dividend yield, EBITDA/EV). The current implementation was chosen for simplicity and is on the fix list at \S\ref{sec:flaws} T1.1. Until fixed, the 15\% weight allocated to value is buying almost no cross-sectional signal.

\textbf{Why AQR uses value:} It is the oldest and most-studied factor (Fama \& French 1993). Tends to work when growth and momentum break (2000--2002, 2022). Its absence here is the strategy's largest current gap.

\subsection{Factor Integration}

\begin{equation}
\text{Score}_i = \text{Mom}_i^{0.35} \times \text{Qual}_i^{0.30} \times \text{Vol}_i^{0.20} \times \text{Val}_i^{0.15}
\end{equation}

Each factor is first converted to a percentile rank in [0,1]. The integrated score is the weighted geometric mean of ranks.

\textbf{Why geometric mean, not arithmetic:} Geometric-mean integration penalises weakness on any one factor. An ETF in the 90th percentile on three factors but the 20th on one ranks below an ETF in the 70th percentile on all four. This ``multi-factor consistency'' requirement --- explicitly the key insight from AQR's factor-integration research --- rejects one-trick candidates in favour of broadly-strong candidates. Arithmetic integration would allow one strong factor to compensate for weakness on the others.

\subsection{Portfolio Construction}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item Score every ETF in the filtered universe.
    \item Rank descending.
    \item Take the top 20.
    \item Assign exponential rank weights: $w_i \propto e^{-\text{rank}_i / 20}$, normalised to sum to 1.
    \item Apply weight bounds: minimum 2\%, maximum 15\%.
\end{enumerate}

Exponential weighting expresses conviction --- the top-ranked position gets meaningfully more weight than the twentieth --- while the bounds prevent single-position concentration. The alternative optimiser (\texttt{OPTIMIZER\_TYPE = "mvo"}) is available in the code but not the live default.

\subsection{Rebalancing}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item Scheduled: bimonthly ($\sim$6 times per year).
    \item Drift-triggered: if any position's actual weight deviates from target by $>$5\%, rebalance.
    \item In practice, drift triggers dominate; scheduled rebalances rarely fire independently.
\end{enumerate}

Drift threshold is deliberately wide. Tighter thresholds cause more trading on smaller deviations --- textbook whipsaw. See \S\ref{sec:principles}.

\subsection{Risk Management}

Every entry BUY places a 10\% trailing stop, GTC, on the position. Trailing stops ratchet up as the position appreciates. On any 10\% pullback from the running high, the stop triggers.

Entry stop-loss of 12\% (a bit wider than the trailing stop) accommodates initial-position volatility before the trailing high-water mark establishes.

Cash reserve of \$70{,}000 is held unallocated. This is a static allocation, not a regime-responsive hedge.

\subsection{Explicit Assumptions}

The strategy assumes:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Factor premia (momentum, quality, low-vol, value) will continue to exist over the holding horizon. They may attenuate but do not disappear.
    \item The rank structure of the universe changes slowly enough that monthly rebalancing captures the signal without significant slippage.
    \item Retail transaction costs at IB ($\sim$5 bps effective) are small enough not to erode the premium.
    \item Long-only holds; no short exposure to hedge market drawdown.
    \item No leverage.
    \item Trailing stops execute at or near their trigger prices. Gap-down scenarios (weekend news, halt reopens) are unhedged.
    \item Position selection ignores tax lot-level implications. Tax-lot management is out of scope.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:principles}

Three non-negotiable filters govern every future proposal:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Deterministic decides.} Every number reaching the broker comes from code in \texttt{src/}. No LLM output influences a size or weight.
    \item \textbf{No magical thinking on knobs.} Every parameter traces to research or a repo-recorded backtest. Knobs whose provenance is intuition go on the research backlog.
    \item \textbf{Slowly varying only.} Whipsaw is evidence of overfitting, not alpha. Long lookbacks are preferred over short. Drift thresholds err high. Regime signals (when added) require hysteresis and minimum dwell time. Factor timing is rejected on principle.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Factor Weights}

The weight vector is momentum $=$ 35\%, quality $=$ 30\%, low-volatility $=$ 20\%, value $=$ 15\%. This is an \emph{informed prior}, deliberately regularised away from any in-sample optimum.

\subsubsection{Why not the in-sample winner?}

The July~2026 diagnostic (grid search over the three-factor simplex, momentum/quality/low-vol, on the cached 2021--2026 history) produced in-sample winners uniformly momentum-heavy: 80--90\% momentum, 0--30\% quality, 10--20\% low-vol. Sharpe up to 1.30 on the 3-year window versus $\approx$0.96 for the current policy.

\textbf{This finding was deliberately not acted on.} Three reasons:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Single-regime evidence.} 2021--2026 was overwhelmingly momentum-friendly (post-COVID rally, mega-cap tech, AI narrative). Both the 5-year and 3-year windows overlap and sample the same regime. This is not an independent invariance test.
    \item \textbf{Non-ergodicity.} Equity returns are multiplicative. For a single investor on a single path, the arithmetic mean of returns diverges from the time-average growth rate they actually experience (Peters 2019). A 50\% drawdown requires 100\% subsequent gain to recover, not 50\%. In-sample-Sharpe optimisation understates this asymmetry. Factors that reduce drawdown variance (quality, low-vol) buy protection against the multiplicative losses a single-path investor most needs to avoid. These are insurance premia, not underperforming factors.
    \item \textbf{Confirmation bias.} The operator is explicitly on record as most concerned about an AI/tech valuation regime unwind. The mechanical winner (90\% momentum) is exactly the exposure that would be worst affected in that scenario. Adopting the in-sample winner would be confirming a bias against the operator's stated risk view.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{The Constraint Framework}

The 35/30/20/15 vector satisfies three constraints:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Ordering: momentum $>$ quality $>$ low-vol $>$ value.} Reflects the ordering of the empirical premia in the AQR literature and the compounding effect that the value implementation is currently a low-fee proxy rather than a true value factor.
    \item \textbf{No factor below 10\%.} A factor below 10\% receives such a small weight in the weighted geometric mean that it fails to discriminate between candidates. The multi-factor-consistency requirement collapses to a three-factor screen.
    \item \textbf{No factor above 40\%.} A factor above 40\% dominates the integrated score to the point that single-factor selection would produce nearly the same portfolio. This defeats the multi-factor rationale and re-introduces single-factor tail risk.
\end{enumerate}

Within those constraints the exact values are round numbers chosen for auditability. They can be reproduced in one sentence to a future maintainer: ``momentum gets the largest weight but capped, quality gets the second-largest, low-vol gets a solid third to hedge whipsaw, value gets the smallest until we fix the proxy.''

\subsubsection{When To Revise}

Revise the weights when at least one of the following is available:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Backfilled history to pre-2020 that permits a non-overlapping regime comparison (2011--2015 vs 2021--2026).
    \item A working value factor using fundamentals (P/E, P/B, dividend yield) rather than expense ratio.
    \item A deliberate operator-declared factor-level sentiment override, committed to \texttt{configs/etf\_smart\_beta.toml} with a dated research note.
\end{itemize}

\section{Current Flaws}
\label{sec:flaws}
%% ============================================================

Adversarial review, tiered by impact. Items remain here until closed; closed items move to the Change Log with the closing commit.

\subsection{Tier 1 --- material design flaws}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{T1.1 The value factor is fake.} \texttt{SimplifiedValueFactor} returns $-1 \times$ expense ratio. This is a fee preference, not a value signal. The 15\% weight buys almost nothing. The natural regime hedge that value provides (2000--2002, 2022) is absent from the model. \textbf{Highest-impact single fix.} Replace with fundamentals composite (P/E, P/B, dividend yield); IB Fundamentals API preferred over yfinance.
    \item \textbf{T1.2 Survivorship bias in the cached universe.} The per-ticker parquet cache contains only ETFs that still trade. Delisted / liquidated ETFs are absent. Every backtest overstates historical performance by the average return of survivors. Mitigation: 1--2\% CAGR haircut on all backtested figures.
    \item \textbf{T1.3 Look-ahead bias presumed absent, never proved.} No pinning test asserts that factor calculations at time $t$ use only data with index $< t$. If any factor uses a window that includes $t{+}1$, the backtest is silently inflated. Fix: add a shift-and-recompute test. Two hours' work.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tier 2 --- significant weaknesses}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{T2.1 No regime awareness.} Strategy runs identically in bull, bear, and crisis regimes. Long-only, concentrated, static 22\% cash reserve, 10\% trailing stops. No signal to de-risk in rising-VIX / breadth-collapsing environments. Fix: add SPY-vs-200d moving average and VIX overlay; when both signals point to stress, shift 100\% target equity to 60\%. Must include hysteresis and $\geq$30-day dwell time in either regime (per Principle 3).
    \item \textbf{T2.2 Correlation blindness in top-N selection.} The integrated score picks the top 20 ETFs by factor score without checking their pairwise correlations. Current live holdings read as a broad international dividend / value / quality tilt with likely 0.6--0.8 pairwise correlations. Effective diversification is materially less than 20 positions suggests. Fix: take top 40 by score, cluster on 6-month correlations, pick top-scored representative per cluster until 20 positions filled.
    \item \textbf{T2.3 Conviction magnitude discarded.} The integrated score's magnitude (0.95 vs 0.62) is not used in the optimiser; only rank order matters. Exponential rank weighting mitigates partially. Fix: pass score magnitude as expected-return alpha to the optimiser.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tier 3 --- smaller nudges}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{T3.1 Factor lookback windows are convention.} 252 / 21 / 60 chosen by AQR convention, never tested for optimality in this universe.
    \item \textbf{T3.2 Risk parameters are round numbers.} 10\%, 12\%, \$70k, 5\% drift, 2--15\% weight bounds --- all defensible, all uncalibrated. Sensitivity grid would locate them on the risk-return frontier.
    \item \textbf{T3.3 Rebalance frequency untested.} Bimonthly vs quarterly turnover trade-off never explicitly measured on this universe.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Priority Order}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item T1.3 (look-ahead pinning test) --- 2 hours. Proves the model is not silently a lie.
    \item T1.1 (real value factor) --- 1--2 evenings. Single highest-impact fix.
    \item T2.1 (regime overlay) --- 1 evening. Drawdown protection before live orders resume.
    \item T2.2 (correlation clustering) --- 1 evening. Effective diversification.
    \item T1.2 (survivorship haircut) --- 1 hour. Honesty in reporting.
    \item T2.3, T3.\* --- as time permits.
\end{enumerate}
